I / IV

The problem: the signal is public, but nobody is watching

Every trading day, two kinds of privileged information leak into public view. The first leaks slowly: since 2012, the STOCK Act has required every member of the U.S. Congress to disclose their stock trades within 45 days — and some of them are suspiciously good at picking winners. The second leaks in real time: when institutional money moves, it leaves footprints — single enormous trades (“whales”) that stand out against the sea of small retail orders around them.

Both signals are free. Neither is watchable by a human at any useful scale. The congressional record is ~57,000 trades across 430 filers; the live market is 212 symbols’ worth of prints ticking past every session. Ahab is the machine that watches both, every night, and cross-references them: it scores every politician as a trader (tiers S through F, by risk-adjusted return), flags when several high-tier filers converge on the same stock, listens for whale-sized volume anomalies in live flow, and has an LLM write the morning report in plain English — every claim traceable to the data files behind it.

But the reason this project exists isn’t the signal. Anyone can bolt a pattern-detector onto market data and generate predictions; the hard engineering problem is not fooling yourself about whether the predictions work. So Ahab grades every call it makes against what the market actually did, recalibrates its confidence nightly, and publishes its own misses. That discipline — not the whale-hunting — is what this write-up is really about.

Ahab’s defining feature isn’t the signal it finds. It’s that every number it reports has been audited by its own grading system — including the unflattering ones you’ll see below.

II / IV

Architecture: one night, five verbs

The system is three cooperating parts: a batch pipeline that rebuilds the congressional leaderboard from disclosure filings, a live scanner that polls equities and streams crypto looking for concentrated order flow, and an interpreter that turns the night’s outputs into a report a human actually reads over coffee. Everything runs unattended on a schedule; by the time the operator wakes up, the system has scanned, graded yesterday’s calls, recalibrated, and written its narrative.

Score every previous prediction against what the market actually did — from a price the system could really have traded.

Update confidence curves from the full grading history, so a “70%” from the system means 70% — not enthusiasm.

An LLM reads the volume, calibration, and news handoff files and writes the narrative morning report.

The stack, and why each piece earned its place

Every choice below answers to one constraint, adopted on purpose: $0/month of infrastructure. No paid APIs, no cloud bill, no managed database. That constraint wasn’t austerity — it was a forcing function. It ruled out renting complexity and forced the architecture to stay small enough for one engineer to fully understand, test, and audit.

Python
The whole system is one language: collectors, analyzers, ~40 analysis and backfill scripts. For a single-developer research system, ecosystem depth and iteration speed beat architectural variety.
SQLite event store
Detections, executions, volume events, and signal snapshots live in an append-friendly single-file database. One writer, nightly batch cadence, zero server to operate — and a backup is a file copy. A “bigger” database would add ops burden and nothing else.
Alpaca · yfinance · CCXT
Free market-data feeds: REST polling for equities, Binance WebSocket via CCXT for crypto. Redundant sources cover each other’s gaps and keep the cost constraint intact.
launchd scheduling
The machine under the desk is the server. The OS scheduler runs the nightly pipeline; there is no orchestration layer to babysit.
pytest
The grading and calibration paths are covered by regression tests — measurement code is treated as production code, because a bug there corrupts every conclusion downstream.
Markdown-first reporting
Reports are files, alerts go out over Discord/Telegram, and every run logs a manifest of exactly which inputs it read and which reports it wrote. Any number in any report can be traced to the files that produced it.

Safety by architecture, not by promise

Ahab runs in suggest mode: it writes reports; a human decides. Automated paper-trading exists in the codebase but sits behind a single explicit configuration gate, with hard rules preventing any code path from loosening that gate at runtime. Restraint is a design decision here, not a missing feature — execution stays fenced until the calibration data says the signal has earned it.

Here is the architecture thinking through one real call. On July 14 the strongest raw signal in the universe was AAPL: a 13-day institutional selling campaign walking into an all-time-high price. The whale ledger alone scored it 0.763 — but each fragility gate then asked its one question, and the volatility gate found the weakness: the vol regime sat at its 97th percentile, high enough that a “campaign” can be noise wearing a trend. What reached the morning report was 0.421, and the report said trim and watch, not short with conviction.

III / IV

The hardest bug: the night I caught my own backtest cheating

Every prediction system needs a way to score itself against history — a backtest. Ahab’s version runs nightly: make a call after the market closes, wait, then grade the call against what prices actually did. The integrity of every chart on this page rests on that grading loop being honest.

It wasn’t. While building the July ensemble backfill, an end-to-end audit of the grading path turned up a subtle flaw: each prediction’s outcome was measured from that same day’s closing price — a price the system could see when it made the call, but could never have traded, because the market was already closed. Any move that happened in the overnight gap was being silently credited as skill. In machine-learning terms this is look-ahead bias, the same failure mode as training on your test set: the model gets quietly graded on information it couldn’t have had.

The fix itself was one decision: re-anchor every grade to the next day’s opening price — the first price a real order could have filled at. The hard part was what honesty required next: regrade all 4,067 settled predictions under the stricter anchor and publish whatever fell out, knowing it might erase the system’s measured edge entirely.

It cut both ways. The experimental momentum lens lost 2.5 points of hit rate — a real chunk of its “edge” had been an untradeable overnight gap. But the combined ensemble gained 5.5 points: the old anchor had been unfairly penalizing it. One in six verdicts flipped. Stricter measurement didn’t just deflate the numbers — it re-ranked which strategies were actually working.

A backtest that flatters you is worse than no backtest at all.

IV / IV

What this project taught me

  1. Measurement is the product

    The anchor bug proved that the grading code, not the signal code, decides whether anything downstream is true. Signals are validated walk-forward and out-of-sample — never fit and scored on the same window — and measurement code gets tests, audits, and the same rigor as anything that ships.

  2. A stated 70% has to mean 70%

    Every call carries a confidence score, and every matured call tests it. Plotting stated confidence against realized win-rate gives the system’s honesty curve — and it shows the ordering is right while the slope is too shallow: a stated 68% currently cashes in at 55%. That gap is measured nightly and feeds recalibration. I’d rather publish this curve than flatter it.

  3. The edge worth claiming is self-knowledge

    Pooled across all 3,993 matured calls, the system’s drift above a coin flip is 1.0σ — luck territory. But split the same calls by the system’s own stated confidence and its top quartile climbs out of the luck envelope at 2.9σ while the bottom quartile sinks. The honest claim was never “it beats the market”; it’s narrower and more useful: the system knows which of its own calls to trust.

  4. Constraints breed engineering, and null results are results

    The $0/month rule produced a simpler, fully auditable system rather than a cheaper copy of an expensive one. And when I built a chart expecting to expose politicians hiding good trades behind late disclosures, the data declined — chronic late filers actually do slightly worse. The finding shaped the design anyway: the radar weights who is buying and ignores lateness entirely.

What’s next

Each item below is queued because the measurement work above earned it a place, not because it would demo well:

  • A consensus lens for the ensemble. The baseline flow and experimental momentum lenses already run side by side every night; the next phase specifies how their agreement — and, more interestingly, their disagreement — becomes a signal of its own.
  • Self-hosted LLM inference. Moving the nightly interpretation step to an open-weights model on a machine under the desk removes the last paid dependency and keeps the $0/month constraint honest end to end.
  • Deeper retroactive calibration. Signal snapshots are persisted precisely so that when grading rules improve — as the anchor fix proved they do — the entire confidence history can be recomputed under the new rules, not just future calls.
  • Execution stays fenced, deliberately. Paper trading unlocks only when the calibration curve says stated confidence matches reality. The roadmap treats that as a gate to earn, not a feature to ship.
